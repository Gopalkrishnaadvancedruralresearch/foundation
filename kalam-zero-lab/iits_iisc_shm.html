1. Why Closed-Form Analytical Formulas Over FEM in High-Frequency EMI?

In modern structural dynamics, the Finite Element Method (FEM) is widely accepted for low-frequency modal vibrations (1 Hz to 500 Hz). However, in the Electromechanical Impedance (EMI) technique, transducers operate at ultrasonic frequencies ranging from 30 kHz to 1+ MHz. At these high frequencies, standard FEM faces severe physical and computational limitations:

A. The High-Frequency FEM Discretization Crisis

To prevent severe numerical wave dispersion and artificial damping, FEM meshing must strictly satisfy the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) and Nyquist spatial wave sampling condition:

Δx ≤ λmin / 10 = c / ( 10 • fmax )

For aluminum or steel (where Rayleigh and shear wave velocities c ≈ 3,000 m/s), an excitation frequency of f = 1 MHz results in an ultrasonic wavelength of λ = 3 mm. Consequently, the maximum element size cannot exceed Δx ≤ 0.3 mm across the entire structural volume.

  • Massive Degree-of-Freedom Explosion: Meshing a realistic structural specimen alongside a sub-millimeter PZT patch and a 15 μm adhesive bonding layer requires millions of 3D coupled-field solid elements (e.g., SOLID226 in ANSYS).
  • Harmonic Frequency Sweeps are Infeasible for Fast Diagnostics: Inverting or factorizing multi-million-DoF electro-elastic stiffness and mass matrices at 1,000 individual frequency points across a 1 MHz sweep takes hours or days on dedicated computing clusters. In contrast, the closed-form analytical equations evaluate in 15 milliseconds directly in client-side memory.
  • Adhesive Aspect-Ratio Distortion: Meshing an ultra-thin (10–20 μm) adhesive bondline leads to extreme element aspect-ratio distortion, introducing false numerical shear-locking errors that do not exist in reality.

B. Zero Discretization Error in Analytical Continuum Mechanics

The 1D, 2D, and 3D analytical formulations solve the exact partial differential wave equations of elastodynamics (∇ • T = ρ ü) and Maxwell's equations without spatial discretization. The wavenumbers:

κx = ω √(ρ / C11E),      κy = ω √(ρ / C22E),      κz = ω √(ρ / C33E)

are mathematically continuous eigenvalues. As a result, there is zero numerical dispersion. In academic and scientific literature, FEM models are formally verified against the analytical formulations as the reference ground truth, rather than the reverse.

Technical Attribute Closed-Form Analytical Formulations (1D, 2D, 3D) Finite Element Method (ANSYS / COMSOL FEM)
Mathematical Solution Exact continuum solution to boundary-value differential equations. Approximation via piecewise polynomial discretization over an artificial mesh.

2. Analytical Mechanics Formulations

A. 1D Formulation [Liang, Sun, & Rogers, 1993, 1994]

Enforces single-axis expansion along the length l; neglects transverse coupling (T2 = T3 = 0):

Y(ω) = jω (w l / h) [ ε̅33T − d312 Y̅11E + d312 Y̅11E ( Za,1D / (Zs,1D + Za,1D) ) ( tan(κl) / (κl) ) ]

B. 2D Formulation [Zhou et al., 1996; Bhalla & Soh, 2004]

Incorporates planar Poisson ratio (ν) coupling across orthogonal axes (x, y) under biaxial plane stress (T3 = 0):

Y(ω) = 2 jω (w l / h) [ ε̅33T − (2 d312 Y̅E / (1 − ν)) + (2 d312 Y̅E / (1 − ν)) ( Za,2D / (Z̅s,eff + Za,2D) ) ( tan(κl) / (κl) ) ]

C. 3D Directional Sum Impedance [Annamdas & Soh, 2007, 2008]

Removes artificial planar assumptions (T1, T2, T3 ≠ 0), capturing complete triaxial boundary resistance across all six faces:

Y(ω) = jω ∫∫A D • n dA = G(ω) + j B(ω)
Total Boundary Impedance: Zs,3D = Zs,xx + Zs,yy + Zs,zz + Zshear
The High-Frequency Thickness Mode Proof: When physical experiments sweep up to 1 MHz, a dominant thickness dilatation resonance peak appears in the 600–900 kHz range. Because 1D and 2D models enforce T3 = 0, their governing wave equations omit the ∂2uz/∂z2 eigenvalue, rendering them mathematically blind to this peak. The 3D Annamdas-Soh formulation incorporates κz = ω √(ρ / C33E), predicting the thickness peak within 2% to 4% of measured laboratory data.
