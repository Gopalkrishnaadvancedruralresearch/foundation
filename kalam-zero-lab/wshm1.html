SECTION 1

What are Waves & What is a Lamb Wave?

1. What is a Wave?

A wave is simply energy moving through a material (medium) without shifting the material permanently. Think of tossing a stone into a silent pond: ripples spread outward carrying kinetic energy, but the water molecules themselves stay in place after bobbing up and down.

2. What is a Lamb Wave?

When an acoustic or ultrasonic wave travels through a very thin plate (like a sheet of aluminum, an aircraft wing skin, or a bridge girder), it bouncing continuously between the top and bottom free surfaces. This special surface-guided elastic wave is called a Lamb Wave (discovered by Horace Lamb).

3. Where Do Lamb Waves Exist?

Lamb waves exist throughout the entire thickness of thin plate structures. Because they travel across top and bottom surfaces simultaneously, they can travel over long distances to detect internal cracks, surface corrosion, holes, and hidden fatigue damage inside metallic or composite plates.

SECTION 2

Understanding Transducers: Actuators vs. Sensors

🔴 What is a PZT Actuator?

An Actuator converts electrical voltage signals into physical mechanical vibrations using Piezoelectric ceramic wafers (PZT). It acts as the "speaker" that injects ultrasonic Lamb waves directly into the plate surface.

🟢 What is a PZT Sensor?

A Sensor does the exact opposite. It catches passing mechanical wave vibrations in the plate and converts them back into electrical voltage signals so we can read them on an oscilloscope (A-Scan output).

SECTION 5

How to Find Damage Location Using Time-of-Flight ($\text{ToF}$)

Step A: Record Healthy Baseline Signal

First, actuate a clean plate without any damage. You will observe a single primary wave packet arriving at the Sensor at time $t_{\text{direct}}$. This is your Healthy Baseline Signal.

Step B: Observe Damaged Signal Behavior

When structural damage occurs, a portion of the incident wave strikes the defect boundary and scatters backwards. The Sensor catches two distinct pulses:
1. Direct Wave: Arrives directly from Actuator $\rightarrow$ Sensor ($t_{\text{direct}}$).
2. Scattered Echo: Arrives along Actuator $\rightarrow$ Damage $\rightarrow$ Sensor ($t_{\text{echo}}$).

Step C: Calculate Damage Distance (Principle)

Using the wave velocity $c_g$ (e.g., $5.4\text{ km/s}$ in aluminum), calculate total path distance:
$$\text{Distance}_{\text{Total}} = c_g \times t_{\text{echo}}$$ By calculating distances across multiple sensor paths, the exact $(X, Y)$ coordinate of the crack is pinpointed via ellipse triangulation.
